A strategy game built on CR-AI

Envoy. Broker the deal.

You're an independent mediator with ten months and limited political capital. Six parties sit across the table. Each has a real goal, room to bend, and a red line. Some of them you can read from thousands of documents. Some have barely left a trace, and you'll be working from a reconstruction that might be wrong.

How to play
  1. Read the parties. Each card shows trust in you, readiness for a deal, and three hidden cards. Veto parties must sign.
  2. Spend capital. Six points a turn. Listen quietly to reveal cards, open back-channels where you write the message yourself, send aid, bring in guarantors, deploy monitors.
  3. Convene talks once three parties trust you (45+), then propose a framework of up to four clauses. Every veto party plus five of six must accept.
  4. Don't cross a red line. A party that sees its red line in your proposal walks out for two turns.
  5. The world pushes back. Each month brings an event, and violence can spill over from connected conflicts.
Why some cards are dashed

CR-AI's biggest problem is balance: the side without a press office disappears from the data. Envoy makes that visible. Each party's evidence tier comes from how many documents in its own voice exist in the CR-AI dataset.

Documented50+ documents. All three cards start known.
Partial10 to 49. The goal is known; the rest is reconstructed or unknown.
ThinUnder 10. You start from CR-AI's reconstruction, and some reconstructions are wrong.
Under the hood

The model never sets a score. It returns a grade from -2 to +2 through a forced tool call, and the engine caps what that grade can do, so a clever prompt can't talk a party into signing. Daily spend caps and per-visitor limits keep it cheap; if the model is unavailable, parties fall back to scripted replies.
